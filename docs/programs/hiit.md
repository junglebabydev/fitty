# HIIT — research and programme (Oct 2026)

**Verdict.** Strong evidence from many meta-analyses shows that hard intervals raise aerobic fitness (VO2max) a lot versus no exercise and a little versus steady cardio [1][2]. For fat loss, HIIT and steady cardio come out about the same, and short programmes change body fat only a little [3][4]. The usual fat-loss pitch leans on calories burned after the session, which are small [19][20], or on a meta-analysis that has since been retracted [5]. We sell it as a time-efficient fitness tool and nothing more.

Sources were read in this session (PubMed records via NCBI E-utilities, Crossref, and the official pages and PDFs). Every number below comes from an abstract or an official page we opened.

## 1. Who it is for, and who should check first

- **For:**
  - Adults who want more aerobic fitness and have about 60–90 minutes a week.
  - People who can already manage 20 minutes of brisk activity, such as walking fast or easy cycling, without symptoms. This entry bar is a coaching convention.
  - People with a knee or hip condition flag, through the named **low-impact track**. The machine sessions have no impact anyway. The circuit swaps four moves (§4).
  - People with no gym. The circuit day needs only floor space. Machine days have a bodyweight fallback (§3).
- **Not for / talk to a clinician first:**
  - Every item below except heart failure comes from PAR-Q+ 2025 [16]. In PAR-Q+, a "yes" to one of its 7 questions leads to follow-up questions and, if needed, advice from a qualified exercise professional. Holding people back from a self-guided app instead is our stricter coaching convention.
  - Anyone a doctor has told they have a heart condition or high blood pressure, or that they should only exercise under medical supervision [16]. HIIT is used in cardiac rehab, but under supervision with staff on hand [12]. A self-guided phone app is not that setting.
  - Anyone who has chest pain at rest, in daily life or during exercise, or who has lost consciousness or lost balance from dizziness in the past 12 months [16].
  - Anyone with another long-term medical condition, or who takes prescribed medicine for one. They answer the PAR-Q+ follow-up questions first. A "yes" there means further advice from a qualified exercise professional or the ePARmed-X+ [16].
  - Anyone pregnant. PAR-Q+ says to talk to a health professional before becoming more active [16]. The app asks them to talk to their midwife or doctor and does not start.
  - Anyone who had a baby in the last 12 months, unless they have completed the Postpartum series. Running guidance after birth keeps impact out of the first 3 months and asks for no pelvic floor symptoms first [28]. The app suggests the Postpartum series. The 12-month window is a coaching convention.
  - Anyone with a cold, fever or other short illness. Wait until better [16].
  - Anyone aged 45 or over who is not used to vigorous or maximal effort. PAR-Q+ advises a qualified exercise professional first [16]. The app allows a start on the "easy start" setting (§4).
  - Anyone with heart failure. In a 261-patient multicentre trial, supervised HIIT was no better than moderate training, 51% of patients trained below the target, and the authors called its feasibility unresolved [13]. This is out of scope for a self-guided app.

**Dose and frequency.** The trials we use trained 2–3 times a week. In the fat-loss review, studies averaged 3 sessions a week for 10 weeks [3]. The sprint trial [6] and the 4 × 4 trial [10] used 3 a week. The 5-year trial in older adults used 2 a week [11]. ACSM's vigorous target is 3 or more days a week [18]. We schedule 3 sessions a week, with a 2-session option. No trial here tested daily HIIT.

**Intensity.** In low-volume HIIT, the fitness gain tracked how hard the hard part was, not the number of repeats: higher intensity predicted bigger gains, and more sprints did not [8]. We assume the same holds for the longer sessions here; that is a coaching convention. So each work bout must be hard, using the 0–10 scale where vigorous starts at 7 [21]. We ask for 8 of 10 on 30–40 s efforts and 7–8 of 10 on 2–4 minute efforts. That split by effort length is a coaching convention. If someone wears a heart-rate monitor, the 4 × 4 target is 90–95% of max heart rate [10]. Treat that as a guide only, because heart-rate targets from a predicted max have limits [23].

**Progression.** We start with long rests (30 s work, 90 s rest), shorten them (30/60), then move to 40/20. The longer intervals build from 2-minute efforts to the 4 × 4 [10]. Session length grows from about 20 to about 30 minutes. The order of these steps is a coaching convention. Only the end point (the 4 × 4) comes from a trial, and that trial was running with recovery at 70% of max heart rate; ours is on a machine with an easier recovery.

**What to expect, and when.**
- Fitness should rise over 6–12 weeks. In healthy adults aged 18–45, the pooled gain versus no exercise was about 5.5 mL/kg/min [1]. Sedentary men gained 19% in 12 weeks of all-out cycle sprints [6]. Moderately trained men gained 7% in 8 weeks of running intervals [10].
- Body fat may fall a little, but short programmes rarely change it in a meaningful way [4]. Cycling programmes did not reduce fat in one review of overweight adults [3], and two of the three weekly sessions here are usually cycling.
- We say this plainly in the intro. Neither track promises fat change.

**What 8 weeks of this series is not.** The hard minutes run from about 17 a week (week 1) to about 40 (week 8). That is below the WHO's 75 minutes of vigorous activity [24]. The coach should keep any walking or other activity the user already does, not replace it.

## 3. The programme

- **Length:** 8 weeks. **Sessions:** 3 a week, on non-consecutive days. A 2-a-week option drops the circuit day. **Minutes:** 20–29 per session in weeks 1–6. In weeks 7–8 the 4 × 4 day runs 33 minutes, 3 over the owner's 30-minute direction. That is the cost of 4 × 4 min with 3 min recovery, the work-to-rest pattern of the Helgerud running protocol [10]. The yaml shows the real total and does not round it down.
- **Equipment:**
  - Day 1 and day 3 need one cardio machine: stationary bike, air bike, rowing machine, elliptical or ski erg. The default is `stationary_bike`.
  - Day 2 (circuit) needs floor space only.
  - No machine: day 1 and day 3 use the bodyweight fallback (below).
- **Session shapes used:**
  - `intervals`: work/rest × rounds, on the machine. Weeks 5, 6 and 8 repeat the block as 2 sets, with 3 minutes easy between sets (`sets`, `setRestSec`).
  - `circuit`: stations × rounds, bodyweight.
  - `steady`: warm-up and cool-down.
  - Warm-ups, cool-downs and rests carry `role: warmup | cooldown | rest`. They are not logged and do not count in the PRD's "no id twice in one session" test. Only work bouts and circuit stations are logged. The role flag is a pending PRD §5.1 addition.
- **Tracks:**
  - `standard`: may include impact moves (`jumping_jack`, `high_knees`, `burpee`).
  - `low_impact`: no id tagged `impact`, `deep_knee_flexion` or `knee_load`.
  - Days 1 and 3 are `both`, because the machines carry no safety tags. Day 2 is written out twice, once per track.

**How minutes are counted:**
- An interval block is `sets × rounds × (work + rest) + (sets − 1) × setRestSec`, with `sets` 1 unless stated. The last rest is easy movement that flows into the cool-down. In the 4 × 4 sessions it *is* the cool-down.
- A circuit is `rounds × (stations × work + (stations − 1) × restBetweenStations) + (rounds − 1) × restBetweenRounds`.

## 4. Safety

**Pre-start screen.** Six questions, paraphrased from PAR-Q+ 2025 [16] and the ACSM screening model [15]. Ask them once at the start, and again after any 21-day gap. The action on each "yes" is ours. It is stricter than PAR-Q+, where a "yes" leads to follow-up questions, and it is a coaching convention.

1. **Has a doctor told you that you have a heart condition or high blood pressure, or that you should only exercise with medical supervision? Do you get chest pain at rest, in daily life or when active? In the past 12 months, have you fainted or lost balance from dizziness?** (Not dizziness from over-breathing during hard exercise.) The two PAR-Q+ items share one question because both have the same outcome; the PRD allows at most 6 questions.
   Yes (`wait`): do not start. The app shows "Please check with your doctor first. Interval training is used in supervised heart programmes, and that is the right place to start it." No override in the app. The same sheet carries one line: "Chest pain now? Call emergency services." with the one-tap dial (see stop signs).
2. **Have you had a baby in the last 12 months?**
   Yes (`suggest:postpartum`): do not start this series; suggest the Postpartum series. Running guidance after birth advises low-impact exercise for the first 3 months, and no running before 3 months or while pelvic floor symptoms are present [28]. Exception: a user who has completed the Postpartum series can start, on the low-impact track unless its symptom check before running was passed (`runCheckPassed`) (P7, P12). The 12-month window, and applying running guidance to jumps and burpees, are coaching conventions.
3. **Are you pregnant?**
   Yes (`wait`): do not start this series. Show "Please talk to your midwife or doctor about exercise in pregnancy." The Postpartum series is for after the birth and does not start in pregnancy either.
4. **Do you have another long-term medical condition, or take prescribed medicine for one?**
   Yes: show "Check with your doctor or a qualified exercise professional first." The series unlocks when the user confirms they have been cleared. The app stores that confirmation with the date. It does not ask which condition.
5. **Do you have a bone, joint or muscle problem from the past 12 months that more activity could make worse?**
   Yes: default to the low-impact track. Ask which area and set the matching knee, hip, back, neck or shoulder condition flag if not already set. If it hurts now, suggest a physio check first.
6. **Are you 45 or over and not used to hard, breathless exercise? Or have you done little planned exercise in the past month?**
   Yes: set `easyStart` (P6). The app suggests talking to a qualified exercise professional, as PAR-Q+ advises for the over-45 case [16]. The "past month" trigger and the effort cap are coaching conventions.

Also: if they have a cold or fever today, skip today's session and do not count it as missed [16].

**Stop signs (during or after a session).** Exercise-related heart events are often preceded by warning signs [15]. The list below is standard exercise-safety practice, not taken from [15]. The timings in it ("a few minutes", "about 3 minutes") are coaching conventions. On any of these the coach says "Stop now", and the timer pauses and does not resume on its own.
- **Chest pain, pressure or tightness. Pain spreading to the arm, jaw or back. Fainting, or nearly fainting.**
  Stop and sit or lie down. If it does not ease within a few minutes, or there was a faint, show "Call emergency services now" with a one-tap dial. Log the stop sign; P8 applies.
- **A racing, pounding or irregular heartbeat that does not settle with rest. Unusual breathlessness that does not ease within about 3 minutes of easy movement.**
  Stop the session. Advise seeing a doctor before the next one. P8 applies.
- **Dizziness or light-headedness.**
  Keep moving gently; do not stop dead on the bike. Sit if it continues. If it recurs in a later session, treat it as the sign above.
- **Sharp joint pain, or pain that changes how you move.**
  Stop that move. Switch to the low-impact track for the rest of the session. Log it to the symptom check-in, which sets the gate.
- **Feeling sick, or a headache that builds during effort.**
  Stop the hard rounds and finish with easy spinning only.

**Condition flags.**
- **Knee or hip flag (owner decision 2026-10-02):** a standing flag is history, not today's symptom. It keeps avoiding `deep_knee_flexion` (so `bodyweight_squat` becomes the glute bridge) but no longer removes `impact`. The standard track runs knee-checked (P13): next-morning knee or hip pain of 4/10 or more moves the next sessions to the low-impact track until the score is back to 3/10 or less.
- **Knee or hip AMBER or RED today, or the user's no-impact choice:**
  - The hip AMBER set is the same as the knee one (`impact`, `deep_knee_flexion`), so both get the designed track rather than the library's substitution chains.
  - The low-impact track is used (P7). It removes `jumping_jack`, `high_knees`, `burpee` and `bodyweight_squat`, in place of `step_jack`, `march_in_place`, `shadow_boxing` and the glute bridges.
  - Machine days are unchanged; none of the machines carries a safety tag.
  - Copy must be honest. The low-impact track shares both machine days with the standard track and swaps only the circuit, so its fitness work is close to the same. On both tracks two of three sessions are usually cycling, which did not reduce body fat in one review of overweight adults [3]. Neither track promises fat change.
  - Bike cue: seat high enough for a slight knee bend at the bottom.
- **Knee RED today:** the low-impact track has no `knee_load` ids, so the gate allows it. As a convention (P11), we cap effort at 7 of 10 that day and swap the circuit for that week's day-1 bike session.
- **Hip RED today:** the RED set adds `axial_load`. No id in this series carries it, so the gate removes nothing beyond the low-impact track. No extra effort cap is set.
- **Back flag:**
  - No id in this series carries `spinal_flexion`, `axial_load` or `spinal_load`, so the gate removes nothing. We do not pretend the series is "back-safe".
  - The machine default is `stationary_bike`, not `rowing_machine`, because rowing repeats a forward bend at the catch. This is a convention and the user can change it.
  - Plank and mountain climber cue: "hips level, no sag".
- **Neck flag:**
  - The only id here tagged `overhead` is the proposed `jumping_jack` (standard track). The gate removes it for neck-flag users, and `step_jack` (arms to shoulder height, untagged) takes its place.
  - `ski_erg` starts each stroke with the hands overhead but is untagged in the library, so the tag-driven gate would not stop it.
  - Until the library tag is reviewed, the series removes `ski_erg` from the machine choice for neck-flag and shoulder-flag users (`overheadFlagExcludes`).
  - Push-up and plank cue: "eyes to the floor just ahead of your hands".
- **Shoulder flag:**
  - The shoulder group removes only `overhead`, and its RED set is empty. So the gate removes `jumping_jack` (standard track; `step_jack` takes its place) and nothing else. `overheadFlagExcludes` also takes `ski_erg` off the machine list.
  - `push_up`, `incline_push_up`, `plank`, `mountain_climber` and `burpee` all put weight through the arms and carry no shoulder tag. The gate does not touch them, and we do not call the series shoulder-safe.
  - If a station hurts, the sharp-pain stop sign applies: stop that move and log it to the symptom check-in.

## 5. In-app intro copy

- **Title:** HIIT
- **Promise:** Raise your fitness with three short, hard sessions a week.
- **Description:** Eight weeks of interval training: two machine sessions and one bodyweight circuit each week, 20 to 33 minutes each. You work hard in short bursts and recover in between. Expect better fitness within weeks. Do not expect much change in body fat; short programmes rarely shift it. Knee or hip issues? The low-impact track keeps every move off the jump.
- **Why it works:**
  - Hard intervals raise aerobic fitness more than no exercise, and slightly more than steady cardio [2].
  - Sessions of 15 minutes or less improved fitness about as much as longer steady cardio [8].
  - In an 8-week running trial, 4 × 4-minute hard efforts raised fitness 7% [10].
- **What you need:** a bike, air bike, rower, elliptical or ski erg for two sessions; a mat-sized space for the third. No machine? We swap in bodyweight intervals.
- **Time per week:** about 65 minutes in week 1, rising to about 90 in week 8.
- **Length:** 8 weeks.

## 6. Coach cues

**Session start**
- "Easy spin first. Five minutes, just warming up."
- "Hard means a word or two. Easy means sentences."
- "Today: six short efforts. Long rests. You've got this."

**Machine work (`stationary_bike`, `assault_bike`, `rowing_machine`, `elliptical`, `ski_erg`)**
- `stationary_bike`: "Seat high, slight bend in the knee at the bottom."
- `stationary_bike`: "Spin faster first, then add resistance."
- `assault_bike`: "Push and pull. Arms and legs share it."
- `rowing_machine`: "Legs, then body, then arms. Reverse on return."
- `elliptical`: "Whole foot on the pedal. Stand tall."
- `ski_erg`: "Hinge at the hips, drive the handles past them."
- 4 × 4: "Settle in. Hard, but you could hold five minutes."
- 4 × 4: "Halfway. Same pace, steady breathing."

**Circuit moves**
- `jumping_jack`: "Light feet, soft knees on landing."
- `step_jack`: "Step out, arms to shoulders, step in. Keep it brisk."
- `bodyweight_squat`: "Sit back, chest up, knees follow the toes."
- `glute_bridge`: "Squeeze the glutes, ribs down, pause at the top."
- `single_leg_glute_bridge`: "Hips level. Switch legs at halfway."
- `mountain_climber`: "Shoulders over hands, hips level, knees drive."
- `push_up`: "Body in one line, chest to the floor."
- `incline_push_up`: "Hands on the bench, body in one line."
- `high_knees`: "Quick feet, knees to hip height, land softly."
- `march_in_place`: "Knees up, arms swinging, stay tall."
- `shadow_boxing`: "Fast hands, loose shoulders, keep breathing out."
- `plank`: "Elbows under shoulders, squeeze glutes, breathe."
- `burpee`: "Step back if you need to. Clean beats fast."

**Rests**
- "Easy now. Let the breathing come down."
- "Ten seconds. Get ready to go."
- "Next up: mountain climbers. Hips level."
- "That round counted. Two to go."

**Finish**
- "Done. Three easy minutes to bring it down."
- "Session logged. How hard was it, zero to ten?"
- "Another week in the bank. Rest day tomorrow."

## 8. Claims we will not make

- **"HIIT burns more fat than cardio" / "28.5% more fat loss."** The meta-analysis behind the 28.5% figure was retracted in December 2020 [5]. The two older meta-analyses found no difference between HIIT and steady cardio [3][4].
- **"Afterburn: keep burning calories for 24–48 hours."** In the studies one review covered, calories after exercise were only 6–15% of the exercise's own oxygen cost, though the figure may be higher for interval work [19]. HIIT added about 35 kJ (about 8 kcal) over steady exercise in studies measuring up to 3 hours, and about 130 kJ (about 31 kcal) in studies measuring longer [20]. Both are small next to the session itself.
- **"Boost your metabolism."** Same evidence as above. The review called the idea that after-exercise burn matters for weight loss generally unfounded [19].
- **"Tabata: 4 minutes beats an hour."** The original protocol was 7–8 efforts at about 170% of VO2max, to exhaustion, on a braked cycle ergometer, 5 days a week, in seven people [9]. A 20/10 bodyweight circuit is not that. We do not use the word.
- **"One minute of exercise is all you need."** The trial used 3 all-out 20-second sprints at about 500 W inside a 10-minute session, 3 times a week for 12 weeks, in 9 sedentary men [6]. That is a narrower claim.
- **"Fat-burning zone."** The fat-loss reviews found no difference between hard intervals and steady moderate cardio [3][4]. Neither supports a special zone.
- **"Toning" or "spot reduction" (for example, "mountain climbers for a flat stomach").** No trial here measured local fat loss from a move, and we do not claim it.
- **"Low-impact HIIT gives the same fat loss."** In one review of overweight adults, cycling programmes (interval or steady) did not reduce body fat [3]. We promise fitness, not fat loss, on both tracks.
- **"HIIT is dangerous for your heart" or "HIIT is safe for everyone."** Both are wrong. Events were rare even in heart patients, but those sessions were supervised [12]. Screening exists for a reason [15][16].
- **"HIIT raises cortisol and stores belly fat."** Not tested here; the house rule bans cortisol explanations.
- **"Do HIIT every day."** The trials this series is built on used 2–3 sessions a week [3][10][11]. The original Tabata study used 5 days a week in seven people [9]. Nothing here supports daily HIIT.
- **"HIIT is more fun, so you will stick with it."** People enjoyed it as much or slightly more straight after a session, but long-term adherence is unknown [25].
