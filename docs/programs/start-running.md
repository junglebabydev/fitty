# Start Running — research and programme (Oct 2026)

**Verdict.** A run/walk plan that builds to 30 minutes of easy running over 9 weeks is sound, and large cohorts link any regular running, even once a week at slow speed, to lower risk of early death [8][9]. The injury evidence is weaker than it is often presented: a slower plan built on the "10% a week" rule did not cut injuries in a trial [3], and about 1 in 5 or 6 beginners in trial plans still got hurt [3][6]. The best current signal is per run: in a cohort of experienced adult runners, a run more than 10% longer than their longest in the past 30 days was linked to more overuse injury [2]. Where the NHS plan differs from that signal is **the big single-run jump**: it goes from 9 to 16 running minutes per run between weeks 3 and 4 [1]. So this programme never steps more than 10%.

## 1. Who it is for, and who should check first

- **For:**
  - adults who can walk briskly for 30 minutes without pain and want to start running;
  - people who have not run regularly in the last 6 months, or never have;
  - anyone with 3 slots of 30–40 minutes a week, on non-consecutive days;
  - people with a knee or hip condition flag and no pain when they walk: they run, on **knee-checked running** (§4), which keeps running only while next-morning pain stays at 3/10 or less;
  - anyone who would rather not run: the same structure without impact (the **walk-first path**, §4), chosen by the user and changeable at any time;
  - people who passed the symptom check at the end of the Postpartum series (`runCheckPassed`). They start at week 1, and `pf-daily` and the postpartum check-in stay on (§4).
- **Not for, or talk to a clinician first:**
  - Chest pain, fainting or unusual breathlessness during exertion, or a known heart, kidney or metabolic condition. Get clearance before starting [15].
  - Pregnant. This series does not start; talk to your midwife or doctor about exercise in pregnancy.
  - Gave birth in the last 12 months, and have not passed the Postpartum series' symptom check. Use the Postpartum series first; it ends with that check.
  - Pain in a knee, hip, ankle, foot or shin that is there when you walk. Get it checked before running or walking for exercise. Meanwhile the series runs on a bike only (the **bike-first path**, §4), not the walk-first path.
  - Surgery on a leg, hip or the spine in the last 3 months. Get clearance from the surgeon or physio first.
  - A bone stress injury (stress fracture) in the last 12 months. Get clearance first; previous injury was the one risk factor that stood out in [7].
  - Already runs 30 minutes without stopping. This series is outgrown.

**Dose and frequency.** Three runs a week with a rest day between is the structure of the NHS plan [1]. It is a convention, not a tested optimum. For health, dose matters less than doing it at all. In a pooled analysis of 232,149 people, any running was linked to 27% lower all-cause mortality, with no clear extra benefit from running more often, longer or faster [8]. In 55,137 adults, running under 51 minutes a week, or slower than 6 mph, was each enough to be linked to lower risk [9]. Both are observational: runners differ from non-runners in many ways. By week 9 the programme gives about 115 minutes a week of activity including warm-ups (3 × 38–40 min). If your running feels moderate rather than vigorous, add one 35-minute brisk walk to reach the WHO floor of 150 minutes [14].

**Intensity.** Every run is easy. "Easy" means you can still talk in full sentences. The talk test tracks the first ventilatory threshold well, and an RPE of 10–11 sits around the same point [13]. That review is narrative, not systematic, so treat the exact numbers as a guide. A beginner who cannot talk while running is going too fast: slow to a shuffle or walk. The walk breaks exist for this.

**Progression.** This is where the evidence is thinnest and most often oversold.
- The "10% a week" rule was tested in a trial of 532 novices. A gentler 13-week plan built on it had the same injury rate as a standard 8-week plan: about 1 in 5 in both [3].
- An exploratory cohort of 874 novices found more distance-related injuries after a weekly rise of over 30% across 2 weeks. The result was not statistically significant [4].
- The strongest current signal is per run, not per week. In 5,205 runners followed for 18 months (588,071 runs), a run more than 10% longer than the longest of the past 30 days was linked to a higher overuse-injury rate [2]. The hazard ratios were 1.64 for a 10–30% spike, 1.52 for 30–100% and 2.28 for over 100%. That reads as a threshold above 10%, not a smooth dose-response. Week-to-week change showed no link.

Three caveats on [2]. The cohort was not beginners: median 9.5 years of running, mean age 45.8, 22% women, all wearing Garmin watches. The measure was **distance**. We cap **running time** per session instead, which is our extrapolation: at a steady easy pace, time tracks distance. We still use the cap because it is cheap, testable and the best signal available. "Up to 10%" was the reference group in [2], so a step of exactly 10% is allowed. How fast each continuous stretch of running grows is not covered by any study we found. Those steps are coaching convention.

**What to expect, and when.** If no week is repeated, 27 runs over 9 weeks take you from 8 minutes of running per session to 30 minutes non-stop. Many people repeat a week or two, and that is the plan working, not failing. 30 minutes is the goal, not 5 km. Covering 5 km in 30 minutes needs 6:00 per km. At an easy 7:00–8:30 per km, 30 minutes covers about 3.5–4.3 km. Expect some aches. In trials of 8–13-week beginner plans, about 15–21% of novices had an injury that limited their running for at least a week [3][6]. Per hour of running, beginners get hurt more than twice as often as regular runners [5]. When novices do get hurt, the median recovery among those who recover is about 10 weeks [18]. A rule-bound slow plan exists to keep you out of that group, but no plan can promise it.

**Shoes and feet.** You do not need a gait analysis or a "stability" shoe. In 927 novices in neutral shoes, moderately pronated feet were not injured more than neutral feet [11]. Pick the pair that feels most comfortable [12]. That advice comes from a narrative review, so it is expert opinion, not trial evidence.

**Knees.** In a meta-analysis of 114,829 people, recreational runners had a lower prevalence of hip and knee osteoarthritis than non-runners (3.5% vs 10.2%) [10]. But the odds ratio against non-runners was 0.86 (95% CI 0.69–1.07), which crosses 1, and the studies were observational and could not show cause. We say "recreational running is not linked to more knee wear". We do not say running protects knees.

## 3. The programme

- **Length:** 9 weeks (longer if weeks are repeated).
- **Sessions per week:** 3, never on consecutive days.
- **Minutes per session:** 26–40, including a 5-minute brisk-walk warm-up and a 5-minute walk cool-down.
- **Equipment:** running shoes that feel comfortable, a phone for the timer. Outdoors, or `treadmill_jog` / `treadmill_walk` on a treadmill.
- **Session shapes used:** `intervals` (run/walk rounds) for weeks 1–8; `steady` (continuous minutes) for warm-up, cool-down, the last stretch of each week-8 session and all of week 9.
- **Standalone workout (PRD §4.9):** w1d1 as "First run/walk", fact "8 × 1 min runs". With a knee or hip flag it opens as w1d1 under knee-checked running; if the user has chosen not to run, as the walk-first w1d1; with pain on walking, as the bike-first w1d1.
- **Structure of each week:** runs 1 and 2 are the same session; run 3 is one step up. The next week starts one step higher again. That gives 17 steps from 8 to 30 minutes of running, each at most 10% more running time than any session before it.

### Week-by-week overview

Times are mm:ss. Every session adds a 5-minute brisk walk before and a 5-minute walk after. In each interval round the walk follows the run, so the last walk leads into the cool-down.

| Week | Runs 1 and 2 | Run 3 | Run minutes (1–2 → 3) | Session minutes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8 × (1:00 run / 1:30 walk) | 7 × (1:15 run / 1:45 walk) | 8 → 8.75 | 30 / 31 |
| 2 | 4 × (2:00 run / 1:30 walk) + 1:30 run / 1:30 walk | 4 × (2:30 run / 1:30 walk) | 9.5 → 10 | 27 / 26 |
| 3 | 4 × (2:45 run / 1:30 walk) | 4 × (3:00 run / 1:30 walk) | 11 → 12 | 27 / 28 |
| 4 | 4 × (3:15 run / 1:30 walk) | 4 × (3:30 run / 1:30 walk) | 13 → 14 | 29 / 30 |
| 5 | 3 × (5:00 run / 2:00 walk) | 3 × (5:30 run / 1:30 walk) | 15 → 16.5 | 31 / 31 |
| 6 | 3 × (6:00 run / 2:00 walk) | 3 × (6:30 run / 1:30 walk) | 18 → 19.5 | 34 / 34 |
| 7 | 3 × (7:00 run / 2:00 walk) | 2 × (11:30 run / 2:00 walk) | 21 → 23 | 37 / 37 |
| 8 | 15:00 run, 2:00 walk, 10:00 run | 20:00 run, 2:00 walk, 7:00 run | 25 → 27 | 37 / 39 |
| 9 | 28:00 run | 30:00 run | 28 → 30 | 38 / 40 |

Largest step in running time between any session and the longest before it: exactly 10% (week 3, run 1: 10 → 11 min; week 5, run 3: 15 → 16.5 min). Every other step is smaller. Longest single stretch of running grows 1:00 → 30:00, in steps of up to 64% (week 7: 7:00 → 11:30); those bout steps are coaching convention.

### Full programme

`runSeconds` is the total running time in the session. The cap rule (R4) reads it.

**One id, many blocks.** `brisk_walk` appears up to 3 times per session and `easy_run` twice in w2d1, w2d2 and all of week 8. The PRD data test ("no id appears twice in one session", §5.1) needs the cross-cutting role flag to pass:
- Warm-up and cool-down blocks carry `role: warmup` or `role: cooldown`. They are shown and timed but not logged as sets, so they are exempt from the test.
- Interval `rest` ids are never logged (PRD §5.3: only work bouts are logged), so they are exempt too.
- A timed cardio work id that repeats across work blocks (`easy_run` in w2 and w8; the fast-segment id on the walk-first and bike-first paths) is logged as **one exercise**: the logger already groups sets by `exerciseId`, so its bouts become timed sets of one entry. Total run time is the sum of those sets, which is what `runSeconds` and R4 read. The test allows this repeat only for timed `cardio` ids in work blocks.

## 4. Safety

### Pre-start screen

| # | Question (yes / no) | If yes, the app… |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Has a doctor said you have a heart condition, or do you get chest pain, fainting or unusual breathlessness when you exert yourself? | Does not start the series. Shows "Please get clearance from your doctor first" and keeps it locked until the user confirms clearance [15]. |
| 2 | Do you have a known heart, kidney or metabolic condition (such as diabetes), and are you not currently active? | Does not start. Asks the user to check with their clinician, then confirm clearance [15]. |
| 3a | Are you pregnant? | `wait`. Does not start. Shows "Please talk to your midwife or doctor about exercise in pregnancy." |
| 3b | Have you had a baby in the last 12 months? | `suggest:postpartum`, unless `postpartum.runCheckPassed` is true. A graduate starts here at w1d1 (R8 not offered), with `pf-daily` and the postpartum check-in kept on, as the Postpartum handoff promises. |
| 4a | Do you have pain in a knee, hip, ankle, foot or shin when you walk? | `path:bike-first` (below). Shows "Get this checked before you run or walk for exercise. Until then, your sessions are on a bike." |
| 4b | Do you have a knee or hip condition flag? (pre-answered from the profile) | `path:knee-checked` (below): running continues while next-morning pain stays at 3/10 or less. The result card also offers "I'd rather not run", which sets the user's no-running choice and `path:walk-first`. |
| 5 | Have you had a running injury in the last 12 months? | Starts at w1d1 whatever the user's fitness (R8 is skipped). Shows: "A past injury roughly doubled injury risk in one study. Start at week 1." [7] |
| 6 | Have you run 10 minutes or more without stopping in the last 30 days? | Asks for the longest run in minutes and applies R8 to choose the starting session. |

If more than one answer is yes, the first `wait` wins, then 3b, then 4a over 4b. Q5 and Q6 still apply to whichever path starts.

**Postpartum graduates.** This series does not repeat the postpartum checks; the Postpartum series owns them. After every session the postpartum check-in still runs, and its rules in `postpartum.md` §4 decide what a sign means. This series reads one result: while those rules block impact, every run segment uses the walk-first mapping (below), and running resumes when they no longer do. R4 is unchanged.

### Stop signs

| During or after a session | What the app tells the user |
|---|---|
| Chest pain or pressure, fainting or near-fainting, a racing or irregular heartbeat, breathlessness out of proportion to the effort | "Stop now and sit down. If it does not settle within a few minutes, call emergency services." Ends the session and locks the series until the user confirms they have seen a doctor. |
| Sharp pain, or pain that makes you limp or change your stride | "Stop running and walk home." Logs the session as stopped early, so R2 repeats the week. |
| Pain at one spot on a bone (shin, foot) that hurts to press, or gets worse each run | "Stop running and walk home gently. No running or walking for exercise until it is checked. Get this checked this week, sooner if it hurts at rest or at night." Moves the user to the **bike-first path** on `stationary_bike` only (not `elliptical`, which still loads the legs); with no bike, pauses the series. Walking or running returns only after the user confirms it was checked; R4 then sets the entry point. |
| A knee that swells, locks or gives way | "Stop running. Get this checked before your next run." Pauses the series. |
| Dizzy, sick or confused in the heat | "Stop, get into shade, sip water." Ends the session. If it repeats, treat as row 1. |
| Next-morning pain 4/10 or more | No alarm. R2 or R3 applies automatically and the coach explains which. |

All stop signs are coaching convention and standard first-aid practice, not trial findings.

### Condition flags, knee-checked running, and the walk-first and bike-first paths

**Owner decision, 2026-10-02.** A standing knee or hip flag records a *history* of trouble. It no longer removes running by itself. The flagged user runs, and running continues only while next-morning pain stays at 3/10 or less. A user who does not want to run can say so, and gets the walk-first path until they change it. This replaces the earlier rule, under which the gate's AMBER set (`impact`, `deep_knee_flexion`), applied permanently by the flag, removed all running.

- **Why this is defensible.** In an RCT of 38 people with Achilles tendinopathy, those who kept running and jumping under a pain-monitoring model did as well as those who stopped [21]. That is a different tissue and a different population, so it is indirect support, and the 3/10 next-morning ceiling is our coaching convention (the trial allowed more). Recreational running was not linked to more hip or knee osteoarthritis than being sedentary [10]. Neither source tests runners with a past knee problem specifically; the rule is a monitored trial, not a promise.
- **Knee-checked running** (`path:knee-checked`; knee or hip flag, Q4a no, no user ban):
  - The standard running sessions and rules R1–R8 apply unchanged.
  - **R9 (flag guard).** The next-morning score (R7) also asks where it hurts. If the flagged knee or hip scores 4/10 or more, the next session runs as its walk-first version, and every session after it, until a next-morning score after a session is 3/10 or less again. Then running resumes at the week R2 or R3 set. A score of 6/10 or more also shows R3's "get it checked" line.
  - **On the day:** the pre-workout symptom gate still runs. If today's knee or hip is AMBER or RED (pain 3/10 or more, swelling, or a red flag), `impact` is avoided for that session, so run segments become the walk-first or bike mapping below. A knee RED day offers `stationary_bike` or a rest day (our proposal, not current gate behaviour).
  - The flag still avoids `deep_knee_flexion`, which nothing in this series uses.
- **The user's choice not to run** (`path:walk-first`): set by "I'd rather not run" on the screen result, by the coach ("I don't want to run", previewed and applied only on a tap), or on the programme page. It applies across the app until the user lifts it (PRD §6.6). The walk-first path:
  - The same 27 sessions, same timings, same rules R1–R8, with R4 counting fast-segment seconds on a separate tally from running. Each `easy_run` segment becomes a fast segment, chosen by equipment in this order: `incline_walk` (treadmill), `stationary_bike` (bike), else `brisk_walk` with effort "fast: breathing harder, short sentences only". Each walk break becomes an easy `brisk_walk`. Week 9 ends with 30 minutes of continuous fast walking.
  - Deterministic mapping, testable: for every block, `exerciseId: easy_run` → the chosen fast id, `seconds` and `minutes` unchanged; warm-up and cool-down (`role: warmup | cooldown`) unchanged.
  - Why it is worth doing: walking at moderate effort counts toward the WHO weekly minutes of moderate activity [14], and more daily steps are linked to lower mortality [17]. The progression itself is coaching convention for walking.
  - If the user lifts the choice, the running path starts where R4 puts it. With no runs in 30 days, that is w1d1.
- Honest line for the intro, flagged users only: "Your knee history doesn't rule out running. We check it each morning after a run and step back if it complains."
- **Bike-first path** (Q4a yes, or the bone-pain stop sign). For pain on walking, whatever the flags:
  - The same 27 sessions, same timings, same rules R1–R8, with R4 on its own fast-segment tally.
  - Deterministic mapping, testable: every `easy_run` work block → `stationary_bike` (else `elliptical`; the bone-pain stop sign allows `stationary_bike` only), effort "fast: breathing harder, short sentences only"; every `brisk_walk` block (warm-up, cool-down, rest) → the same machine, effort "easy, breathing settles"; `seconds` and `minutes` unchanged.
  - The app shows "Get this checked" on every session until the user confirms it was checked. Then: with no pain on walking, the running path starts where R4 puts it (under knee-checked running if a knee or hip flag is set), unless the user has chosen not to run, in which case the walk-first path.
  - Why it is worth doing: moderate cycling counts toward the WHO weekly minutes [14]. The progression is coaching convention.
  - Honest line for the intro: "While walking hurts, you train on a bike. Get the pain checked first."
- **Back** (AMBER avoids `spinal_flexion` and `axial_load`; RED adds `spinal_load`): `easy_run` carries none of these tags, so the gate removes nothing. Back pain counts in the next-morning score (R7), so R2 and R3 still slow things down. Open question: should running carry `axial_load`? It does not today (`treadmill_jog` is `[impact, knee_load]`).
- **Neck** (AMBER avoids `overhead`; RED adds `neck_load`): nothing in the series carries these tags. No change.

## 5. In-app intro copy

- **Title:** Start Running
- **Promise:** From walking to 30 minutes of easy running in nine weeks.
- **Description:** Three runs a week, mixing short runs with walking. Running time grows in small steps, never more than 10% above your longest recent run. If something aches the next morning, you repeat the week. Nine weeks if every week goes well, longer if not. Both are fine.
- **Why it works:**
  - No run adds more than 10%: bigger single-run jumps were linked to more overuse injuries [2]
  - Any regular running, even once a week, is linked to lower risk of early death [8]
  - Easy pace means you can talk in sentences; the talk test is a valid guide to effort [13]
- **What you need:** comfortable running shoes, a phone, a safe route or a treadmill.
- **Time per week:** about 80–120 minutes (3 sessions of 26–40 minutes).
- **Length:** 9 weeks, or longer if you repeat weeks.
- **One honest line (shown under "Why it works"):** Around 1 in 5 or 6 beginners still gets injured in plans like this [3][6]. A slower plan did not help in a trial [3].

## 8. Claims we will not make

- **"The 10% rule prevents injuries."** A trial of a 10%-rule plan found no fewer injuries [3]. Our cap is per run, from a cohort [2], and we call it that.
- **"Run 5 km in 9 weeks."** The goal is 30 minutes. 5 km in 30 minutes needs 6:00 per km, which many beginners will not run at an easy pace.
- **"Our plan prevents injury."** About 15–21% of novices in trial plans were injured [3][6]. We say "lowers the chance of the jumps linked to injury" and no more.
- **"Going slower is always safer."** A 13-week plan was no safer than an 8-week one [3]. We do not stretch the plan for its own sake.
- **"You need gait analysis or stability shoes if you overpronate."** Moderate pronation was not linked to more injury in neutral shoes [11]. Choose by comfort [12].
- **"Stretch before you run to prevent injury."** Stretching did not reduce sports injuries in a meta-analysis of trials [19]. A brisk walk is the warm-up.
- **"Strength training will prevent your running injuries."** The strong effect in [16] comes from athletes aged 12–40, not novice runners. A walking and hopping preparation block did not reduce injuries in novice runners [6]. Strength work is worth doing for its own sake: offer the strength series as a separate choice, not as injury insurance.
- **"Reading these injury tips will keep you safe."** An online prevention-advice programme did not reduce running injuries in recreational runners [20]. The rules do the work, not the tips.
- **"Running ruins your knees"**, and equally **"running protects your knees"**. Recreational running was not linked to more knee and hip osteoarthritis, and the observational studies cannot show cause either way [10].
- **"The more you run, the longer you live."** No dose-response was found; any running was linked to the benefit [8].
- **"Slow running burns more fat."** No source we read supports it, and it is a banned framing in coach copy.
- **"Runs at this pace are 'zone 2'."** We use the talk test [13]. We do not claim a heart-rate zone without a test.
