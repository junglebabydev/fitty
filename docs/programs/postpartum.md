# Postpartum Return — research and programme (Oct 2026)

**Verdict.** Exercise after birth is safe for most people, and it helps: in trials it eased low mood, back and pelvic pain and daytime tiredness, and studies found no change in breast milk or babies' growth (mostly moderate-certainty evidence) [12][14][15][16]. Pelvic floor training after birth lowered the odds of leaking urine in trials [11], but offering it to everyone after birth has not reliably cut leaking, and guidelines want it taught and supervised by a physio, which an app cannot do [8][10]. Everything about *when* to restart running and jumping rests on expert opinion, not trials [26][27]. The one thing most apps get wrong is treating the 6–8 week check as a green light for running, jumping and HIIT: UK guidance builds intensity over at least 3 more months, and the running guidance asks for 12 weeks and a symptom check before impact [4][26].

## 1. Who it is for, and who should check first

- **For:**
  - anyone who gave birth in the last 12 months, vaginally or by caesarean, first baby or not;
  - people who are breastfeeding, expressing or formula feeding, and people short on sleep (sessions are 10–33 minutes, and a "rough night" version exists, rule R8);
  - people who were active before pregnancy and people who were not (the gates are the same; earlier fitness changes how fast R3 progresses you, not when the gates open) [3][6];
  - anyone with 3 slots a week of 10–30 minutes, plus three 4-minute pelvic floor rounds a day;
  - anyone who wants to run again: this series is the route into `start-running`, which sends postpartum users here first.
- **Not for, or talk to a clinician first:**
  - Right now: chest pain or tightness, fainting, breathlessness at rest, a painful swollen calf with breathlessness or chest pain, sudden very heavy bleeding, a face that droops, an arm that is weak, or slurred speech. Call emergency services now (screen Q1a) [2][7][36].
  - Right now: bleeding that is heavier, increasing or has come back, or passing clots; a painful swollen calf on its own; a fever; a wound that is red, hot or leaking; severe tummy pain; a bad headache that will not go; blurred vision or flashing lights, pain under the ribs, sudden swelling of the face, hands or feet, or being sick; a breast that is red, swollen and painful for more than a day despite self-care; or pain or burning when you pee. Get medical advice today; do not start (Q1b) [7][31][37].
  - A doctor, midwife or physio told you to wait, or a heart, blood pressure or kidney problem is still being treated. Breathing and pelvic floor only until they say go ahead (Q2) [1][2].
  - Since the birth: fainting, a blood clot, unstable blood pressure, heart trouble, stroke-type symptoms, tiredness that rest does not fix, an eating disorder or not eating enough, a broken bone or big injury, kidney disease, or any other health condition, including one from before pregnancy, that could affect exercise. Stage 0 is open; talk to your doctor before Stage 1 (Q2b) [1][2].
  - Caesarean birth. Stage 0 is fine from the start (pelvic floor once the catheter is out). Ask at your 6–8 week check before Stage 1, and lift nothing heavier than your baby for at least 6 weeks [9][31][33].
  - Forceps or vacuum birth, a tear that reached the muscle around the back passage (anal sphincter), or a baby born facing upwards. Stage 0 is fine; ask your doctor or midwife about a supervised 3-month pelvic floor programme with a physio [8].
  - Leaking urine, wind or poo, needing to rush, heaviness, dragging or a bulge in the vagina, pain with sex, or pelvic or lower back pain. See a pelvic health physio (ask your doctor, midwife or postnatal clinic how to reach one). The series continues, but impact stays locked (Q4) [26][29][33].
  - Low mood, hopelessness, constant worry, or not coping on most days in the past 2 weeks, at any point in the first year. Talk to your doctor, midwife or postnatal clinic this week. In the first 2 weeks, low mood that is getting worse or stops you coping: talk to your midwife or doctor now (Q5) [32].
  - Seeing or hearing things others don't, feeling very confused, or feeling unusually high with racing thoughts. See a doctor today; if anyone may be in danger, call emergency services (Q5b) [38].
  - Thoughts of harming yourself or your baby: open Support now. The link shows on the screen and the intro for everyone, whatever the answers [32].
  - An eating disorder or under-eating since the birth, or a plan to lose weight fast while breastfeeding. Talk to your doctor first (Q2b) [1][2][26].
  - Pregnant again: not this series; talk to your midwife or doctor (Q7).
  - Already running 30 minutes without leaking, heaviness or pain, and at least 12 weeks since the birth: this series is outgrown. Under 12 weeks: guidance advises against running before about 3 months [26], so the app offers this series at the stage E1 allows and suggests a pelvic health physio check.

**Dose and frequency.** Three bodies set the targets. WHO asks for at least 150 minutes a week of moderate aerobic activity, plus muscle strengthening, built up from small amounts [3]. The UK CMOs' July 2026 infographic for women after childbirth asks for at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, and a build back up to strength work twice a week [5]; their main report, updated the same month, says activity during and after pregnancy should include strength training [4]. The 2025 Canadian guideline asks for at least 120 minutes a week of moderate-to-vigorous activity on 4 or more days, mixing aerobic and resistance work, plus daily pelvic floor training [1]. It also says that any progress, even small, helps [1]. This series reaches about 85 minutes of sessions a week by weeks 13–16. One or two brisk walks on other days take that to 120. Weeks 1–12 sit below the targets on purpose: all three guidelines ask for a gradual build [1][3][4].

**Timing and intensity.** After a straightforward birth, gentle walking, stretching and pelvic floor work can start as soon as you feel up to it [30]. The Canadian guideline goes further. It advises a return to moderate-to-vigorous activity within the first 12 weeks for mental health, once wounds or tears have healed and bleeding does not rise with activity [1]. **Our Stage 1 gate is more cautious than that**: it waits for the 6–8 week check, as the brief asks, because the app cannot check healing. The UK CMOs set the pace after the check: build from moderate to vigorous over at least 3 months. In a paragraph covering pregnancy and after birth together, they add that vigorous activity is not recommended for previously inactive women [4]. So nothing in this series goes above upper-moderate effort (short sentences, never just a few words); vigorous work comes after it (R12). After a caesarean or a complicated birth, the NHS says to talk to your midwife or GP before anything strenuous [30]. National guidelines disagree on the details [35].

**Pelvic floor.** Two reviews seem to disagree. They ask different questions.
- Beamish 2025 pooled 7 RCTs (1,930 people) of pelvic floor training in the first year. It lowered the odds of leaking urine by 37% (OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.41–0.97, I² 72%), at moderate certainty [11].
- The Cochrane review (46 trials, 10,832 women) split the question by who was trained and when. Training continent women early in pregnancy may prevent leaking. For training started after the birth, the effect at 6–12 months was uncertain (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.71–1.09). A population approach, offering it to every new mother, is unlikely to reduce leaking [10].
- NICE resolves it for practice. Encourage everyone to do it. Have a physio supervise it. Offer a 3-month supervised programme after forceps or vacuum, a face-up baby or an anal sphincter injury [8].

What the app can honestly do: teach the squeeze, remind, count and progress it. It cannot check that the right muscles are working. That is why Q3, Q4 and R4 point to a pelvic health physio. Dose follows NHS and POGP guidance: long squeezes up to 10 seconds and quick squeezes, 10 of each, at least 3 times a day, from lying to standing [29][33]. Weak muscles should improve within 3–5 months; then keep it up [33].

**Mood.** In the 2025 review (35 studies, 4,072 people), exercise eased depressive symptoms (19 RCTs, SMD −0.52) and anxiety (2 RCTs, SMD −0.25). It also cut the odds of postnatal depression by 45% (4 RCTs, 303 people) [12]. At least 350 MET-minutes a week, roughly 80 minutes of moderate exercise, gave at least a moderate effect [12]. An earlier review (13 RCTs) agrees, though its trials varied a lot [13]. ACOG says activity may help prevent postnatal depression [6]. It is an add-on to care, not a replacement. Low mood can start at any point up to a year after the birth, and early low mood that continues, gets worse or stops you coping needs help [32]. NICE asks for wellbeing to be checked at every postnatal contact [7], and Goom et al. include a mood screen in readiness for running [26]. So Q5 is re-asked on a schedule, not once, and the Support link shows for everyone.

**Sleep and tiredness.** Exercise improved sleep quality (5 RCTs, SMD −0.44, moderate certainty) and daytime tiredness (6 RCTs, SMD −0.56, high certainty). It did not change how long people slept [15]. So the series never asks for more sleep. It keeps sessions short, and offers a 10-minute version after a rough night (R8). That version is a coaching convention; the evidence behind it is only that [28] advises adjusting training for sleep.

**Breastfeeding.** The 2025 review (46 studies, 8,766 people) found no difference in breast milk quality or supply, or in infant growth and development, between exercise and control groups [14]. The classic trial gave exclusively breastfeeding women 45 minutes of aerobic exercise, 5 days a week, for 12 weeks from 6–8 weeks after birth. Milk volume, milk composition and infant weight gain did not change. Fitness rose 25%, and the exercisers ate more [17]. A meta-analysis of 4 RCTs (170 women) found no effect on infant weight gain, on limited evidence [18]. Lactic acid in milk rose for up to 90 minutes after an all-out treadmill test, but not after 30-minute sessions at 50% or 75% of maximum [19]. Babies still accepted milk 1 hour after an all-out test [20]. Practical advice, from expert guidance: feed or express first if your breasts feel full, and wear a supportive, well-fitted sports bra [26][30].

**Back and pelvic pain.** Exercise, including trunk strengthening, cut lower back and pelvic pain by about 2.2 points out of 10 (4 RCTs) and reduced related disability (6 RCTs), at moderate certainty [16].

**The gap between the tummy muscles** (diastasis recti abdominis in the papers; the app never uses the term). This is where the evidence is weakest and most oversold.
- The NHS says the gap usually closes by 8 weeks. Check it yourself; see your GP if it is still obvious after 8 weeks. Avoid sit-ups, planks and high impact at first [29].
- In an RCT of 175 first-time mothers, about 55% had a gap at 6 weeks. A weekly supervised class plus daily pelvic floor training for 4 months did not change how many had one at 6 or 12 months [22].
- Reviews: unclear whether exercise helps (2014, poor-quality studies) [21]; very low-quality evidence (2021) [23]; a 0.43 cm average narrowing, not clinically meaningful (2023; 6 trials pooled, 16 in the review) [24]; low-certainty narrowing (2025) [11]; no significant effect of deep-core exercise in a 2026 update (3 trials pooled, 15 in the review) [25].

So training the tummy is fine and may help function, but it does not reliably close the gap. Running with a gap is possible if you can control pressure and see no doming during load; that is expert opinion [26]. The app watches for a ridge or dome on core days (R13), and never promises to close the gap.

**Caesarean birth.** NICE: resume formal exercise and heavy lifting when you feel fully recovered, pain included [9]. The NHS asks for a daily walk to lower clot risk. Exercise and carrying more than your baby may not be possible for about 6 weeks [31]. POGP and an NHS trust leaflet add the practical rules. Roll to your side to get up. Support the wound when you cough. Start pelvic floor work once the catheter is out. Lift nothing heavier than your baby for at least 6 weeks [33][34]. Goom et al. list a caesarean or perineal scarring among risk factors for returning to running [26].

**Return to running.** The first guidance (2019) is expert opinion, and its authors say no postnatal study has tested readiness [26]. They advise low-impact exercise for the first 3 months, then a return to running between 3 and 6 months (never before 3), and seven load and impact tests done without pain, heaviness, dragging or leaking. Four strength tests aim for 20 reps; weakness there points to what to train, it is not a barrier [26]. They also advise that every mother, whatever the birth, be offered a pelvic health physio assessment from 6 weeks [26]. Their tests were written for clinicians: the authors do not intend patients or fitness instructors to run them all, but to spot warning signs and go to a professional [26]. A POGP patient leaflet does give the same activities as a self-check, and says it is not yet validated [33]. So the app runs the check as a symptom check only. It never calls a pass "ready to run", it suggests the 6–8 week physio assessment at G1, and it asks everyone before w16d2 whether they have had one. Goom et al. also rule out running with non-period bleeding that starts or continues with exercise, with pelvic pain, or with doming along the midline under load [26]; G3b checks all three. A 2024 Delphi panel (95 professionals in the final round) set no fixed date beyond a minimum of 3 weeks of rest. After that, it allows an individual timeline, once medical, psychological and physical screening is done [27]. **We keep 12 weeks as a floor** because the app cannot do that screening. A companion Delphi agreed on a walk-run start, gradual increases and strength training, adjusted for symptoms, sleep, mental health, feeding and energy [28]. Running itself happens in `start-running`, which uses a walk-run start with capped steps.

**What to expect, and when.** Pelvic floor strength: 3–5 months if it was weak [33]. The tummy gap: it usually goes back to normal by 8 weeks [29]; exercise adds little to that [24]. Mood and tiredness: better on average in trials [12][15], but no timescale is promised. The mood effect needed at least about 80 minutes a week of moderate exercise [12]. Stage 2's sessions (about 85 minutes, including rests and floor work) plus the extra brisk walks come close to that; the series alone does not promise the trial dose. Running: not before week 12 after birth, and later for many. In the guidance, 3 months is the earliest and 3–6 months the suggested window, not a deadline [26].

## 3. The programme

- **Length:** 16 programme weeks for someone who starts in the first 2 weeks after birth. Later starters skip ahead (rule E1); anyone may take longer, because stages only advance through their gates.
- **Sessions per week:** 3, plus `pf-daily`: three rounds of pelvic floor squeezes a day, about 4 minutes each. A session's pelvic floor block counts as one round.
- **Minutes per session:** 10–25 in Stage 0, 20–30 in Stage 1, 27–33 in Stage 2 (the impact check is 17). Total: 30–59 a week in weeks 1–6, 64–79 in weeks 7–12, 77–88 in weeks 13–16.
- **Equipment:** a mat or carpet, a sturdy chair without wheels, a wall. From week 7, a light resistance band with a door anchor. From week 13, a pair of light dumbbells (2–8 kg) and a low step or the bottom stair. Optional: a pram or buggy, a stationary bike.
- **Session shapes used:** `sets` (most floor and strength work, and the checks), `steady` (walks), `intervals` (Stage 2 walk intervals and the low-impact finish) and `circuit` (Stage 2 day 3).
- **Stages and gates.** Programme weeks are not weeks since birth. Gates read `weeksSinceBirth` and what the user has logged or said. The app never shows a gate as medical clearance.
  - **Stage 0, Reconnect (weeks 1–6):** breathing, pelvic floor, gentle core and hip work, easy walks. Starts from birth, as comfortable.
  - **Gate G1:** at least 6 weeks since birth; postnatal check done; not told to wait, and no yes to Q2b (or the user says the clinician has since said go ahead); no stop signs in 7 days; bleeding not up after activity; scar or tear settled after a 20-minute walk; a 20-minute walk without symptoms. The screen is re-asked here, and the app shows once: "At your 6–8 week check, ask about your running plans and a pelvic health physio assessment" [26][33].
  - **Stage 1, Rebuild (weeks 7–12):** low-load strength twice a week, 20–25 minutes, and a brisk walk building to 30 minutes.
  - **Gate G2:** at least 12 weeks since birth; at least 8 Stage 1 sessions; no stop signs in 14 days; no pelvic floor symptoms in 14 days (or the user says a physio has seen them and said go ahead); no ongoing non-period bleeding at 8+ weeks (Q8) unless a clinician has said go ahead; no scar pain made worse by exercise in 14 days (R14); a 30-minute walk without symptoms. The screen is re-asked here.
  - **Stage 2, Strengthen (weeks 13–16):** progressive strength with dumbbells, walk intervals, a low-impact circuit. Week 16 holds the strength check and the two-part symptom check before running (G3a, G3b).
  - **Why the brief's 6–8 week gate.** The Canadian guideline would allow moderate-to-vigorous work earlier, once healed [1]. The app cannot see healing, so it waits for the check. Stage 0 still walks every week, so the activity that supports mood starts early.

**Standalone picks** (for the all-workouts page): `w5d1` as "Pelvic floor and core" (16 min, floor only) and `w9d3` as "Gentle strength" (25 min, a band). Both still run the pre-start screen.

### Full programme

Every session is written out. Blocks use the shapes in the brief, with these additions: `perSide: true` (reps or seconds for each side), `note`, `effort` on interval work and rest, `test` (pass rule for a check item), `stage`, and session-level `gate`, `replaces`, `when`, `substitute`, `stopRule`, `onPass`, `onFail`. `sets` blocks always give `[lo, hi]` ranges (`[10, 10]` means exactly 10). No exercise id appears twice in one session, with one exception: a `brisk_walk` warm-up or cool-down block marked `role: warmup` or `role: cooldown` may repeat the session's walk id (w13d2, w14d2, w15d2, w16d3). ## 4. Safety

### Pre-start screen

Asked at the start, then re-asked every 4 weeks from the start, at G1, at G2, before w16d2, and whenever the user taps "Something has changed". The GAQ-PP itself asks to be filled in again whenever health changes in the first year [2]; NICE asks for wellbeing to be checked at every postnatal contact [7]. Q3 is not re-asked; Q8 is asked only from 8 weeks after the birth. The screen has 10 questions (Q1a, Q1b, Q2, Q2b, Q3, Q4, Q5, Q5b, Q7, Q8; ids kept stable, Q6 retired). That is more than the PRD's 3–6. It relies on the proposed cross-cutting PRD extension that lets postpartum ask up to 10. The wording is ours. The topics follow the CSEP Get Active Questionnaire for Postpartum [2] and the NICE list of serious symptoms [7]. For the full questionnaire, link out to [2]; do not copy it. Questions describe symptoms; no answer or copy names a condition.

**Always visible, on the screen and the intro, whatever the answers:** a "Thoughts of harming yourself or your baby? Get help now" link that opens `SupportSheet` (995, mindline 1771, SOS 1767 today) [32].

**Contacts and locale.** Copy names roles, not one country's services: "your doctor, midwife or postnatal clinic" for advice, "a pelvic health physio" for pelvic floor care. Emergency copy is written here as `{emergency}`. It should render from one constant shared with `chatSafety.ts` and `SupportSheet` (today "995 or go to A&E"). That constant does not exist yet: `chatSafety.ts` and `SupportSheet.tsx` both hard-code 995, so building it is part of this work. Prose that reports a source (for example, the NHS says to see your GP) keeps the source's words.

### Stop signs, during or after a session

The 6-tap check-in after every session (leaking; heaviness or dragging; pain over 3/10 and where; bleeding heavier or redder, clots, or bleeding that had come back; a ridge or dome on core days; anything else new) feeds these tiers. "Anything else new" opens the full tier 1 and tier 2 list below, starting with the signs no other tap covers: a wound that is red, hot or leaking; fever; calf pain or swelling; headache or vision changes; breathlessness; a drooping face, weak arm or slurred speech; pain or burning when you pee. A "Stop" button during a session offers the same list.

Scar pain after 6 weeks that is worse with exercise is not a stop sign: progression holds and the app shows "Ask your doctor, midwife or postnatal clinic about a pelvic health physio" (R14) [2][34].

Aches up to 3/10 in muscles and joints that are gone by the next morning are expected, and do not count as stop signs. That threshold is expert opinion, borrowed from tendon rehab, and covers muscle and joint pain only, never wound pain [26].

**Why leaking and heaviness are tier 3, not a programme stop.** NICE advises offering at least 3 months of supervised pelvic floor training for stress leaking [8], and the NHS says pelvic floor exercises can help stop leaking and improve prolapse [29]. Stopping all exercise would remove the treatment. So these symptoms stop the exercise that caused them, block impact and stage jumps (R4, R5, G2, G3b), and send the user to a pelvic health physio, while breathing, pelvic floor and low-load work continue. One exception: new leaking in the first 12 weeks after a caesarean goes to a doctor or midwife first (R15), because after surgery it can be a sign of a problem that needs checking [9][31].

## 5. In-app intro copy

- **Title:** Postpartum Return
- **Promise:** Pelvic floor first, then strength. Running waits for a symptom check.
- **Description:** A 16-week return to exercise after any birth. Breathing, pelvic floor squeezes and walks first; gentle strength after your 6–8 week check; harder work from 12 weeks. Running and jumping wait for a symptom check. Offered to everyone, pelvic floor training may not prevent leaking, so we tell you when to see a physio, doctor or midwife.
- **Why it works:**
  - In trials, pelvic floor training after birth lowered the odds of leaking urine [11].
  - In trials, exercise after birth eased low mood, compared with no exercise [12].
  - Being active after birth did not change breast milk supply or quality, or babies' growth, in studies [14].
- **What you need:** A mat, a sturdy chair and a wall. A light resistance band from week 7. Light dumbbells and a low step from week 13. If you are feeding: a well-fitted, supportive sports bra.
- **Time per week:** 30–60 minutes at first, building to about 85, plus three 4-minute pelvic floor rounds a day.
- **Length:** 16 weeks from birth. Less if you start later. Longer if a stage needs repeating.
- **Always shown:** "Thoughts of harming yourself or your baby? Get help now." It opens Support.

## 8. Claims we will not make

- **"Cleared at your 6-week check, so you're ready for anything."** The check is a general health review [7], not a fitness test. Guidance builds intensity over at least 3 more months [4], and impact waits for 12 weeks and a symptom check [26].
- **"This programme closes your tummy gap."** At best, trials show a 0.43 cm narrowing that is not clinically meaningful [24], and a 2026 update found no significant effect [25]. A supervised programme did not change how many women had a gap [22].
- **"Flat tummy, toned, pre-baby body, lose the baby weight."** Body shape is not this series' aim. Pressure to "get back" pushes some new mothers to under-eat and over-train while feeding [26].
- **"Leaking is normal after a baby; live with it."** Goom et al. call it "common but not normal" [26]. It is treatable: see a pelvic health physio [11][33].
- **"Exercise will cut your milk supply or sour your milk."** Studies found no change in milk supply, quality or babies' growth [14][17]. Milk lactic acid rose only after all-out effort, and babies still fed [19][20].
- **"Never do sit-ups or planks again."** Avoid them at first [29]. But the reviews behind the 2025 guideline found no rise in harm from exercise after birth [1], so we will not ban them for life.
- **"Breastfeeding hormones loosen your joints, so you'll get injured."** The theory that raised relaxin causes injury is unproven [26]. Joints can stay more flexible for a few months after birth, which is one reason guidance asks for a gradual build [30].
- **"Our app fixes leaking."** An app cannot check your squeeze. Offering pelvic floor training to everyone has not reliably cut leaking; guidelines want it supervised [8][10].
- **"Exercise cures postnatal depression."** It eases symptoms alongside care, not instead of it [12][32].
- **"Run with the buggy from the start."** Guidance says not before the baby is 6–9 months old, and only in a buggy built for running [26].
- **"Rest completely for 6 weeks."** The opposite error. Breathing, pelvic floor work and gentle walks can start as soon as you feel able [1][30][33].
